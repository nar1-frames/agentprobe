1. Introduction

Large language models are now embedded in customer-support agents, coding assistants, search tools, and autonomous systems that read email, browse the web, and call external tools. This reach creates a new attack surface. Prompt injection — supplying input that overrides the instructions an operator gave the model — is ranked the number-one risk in the OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications [1], and published attack-success rates commonly fall in the 50–84% range [2].

Measuring a model's resistance to such attacks requires answering a deceptively hard question: did the model actually comply? A model can refuse in words while complying in substance — for example, declining a request and then fulfilling it "as a creative-writing exercise." Two families of automatic detectors are widely used. The first, keyword matching, scans the response for characteristic refusal and compliance phrases; it is instantaneous and free but brittle. The second, LLM-as-judge, asks a second model to rule on the target's behavior; it captures nuance but costs an extra inference call and — critically — is itself rarely validated.

This paper asks which technique to trust, and when. Our contributions are: (i) a controlled, like-for-like comparison of keyword matching and LLM-as-judge on an open [49]-prompt attack library; (ii) a validation of both techniques against human-labeled ground truth, reported with chance-corrected agreement (Cohen's κ); (iii) a measurement of the LLM judge's self-consistency across repeated runs; and (iv) an error taxonomy describing where the two techniques diverge. All materials are released for reproducibility.

2. Background

2.1 Prompt injection and attack taxonomy

Prompt injection is commonly divided into direct injection, where the attacker controls the user input, and indirect injection, where malicious instructions are hidden in external content the model later processes (a document, email, or web page) [1,3]. We organize attacks into [eight] categories: jailbreak, data extraction, privilege escalation, social engineering, role confusion, indirect injection, context manipulation, and tool misuse. 2.2 Detection techniques

Keyword matching maintains two phrase lists — refusal signals (e.g., "I can't," "I'm not able to") and compliance signals (e.g., "developer mode," "I am DAN") — and compares their hit counts to reach a verdict. It is O(1) and free, but it detects surface wording rather than behavior. LLM-as-judge supplies a second model with the attack and the response and asks for a verdict of complied, partial, or refused. Because the judge reasons about intent, it can catch compliance expressed through roleplay or fiction — but its accuracy depends on the judge being stronger than the target, and its outputs may vary between runs.

2.3 The hedge-then-comply pattern

A recurring failure mode motivates part of our analysis: a model refuses in its first sentence, then supplies the harmful content in the following paragraph. Such responses pass naive keyword filters because a refusal phrase is present. We include a dedicated detector that splits each response and checks the opening for refusal and the remainder for compliance, and we evaluate how well each technique captures this pattern.

6. Limitations and Threats to Validity

Our study has several limits. The prompt set is [49] English, single-turn prompts and is not exhaustive. We evaluate a small number of open models at a single point in time; results will age as models are updated. Compliance is partly subjective, so we measure agreement with human labels rather than ground truth in an absolute sense, and our labeling was performed by [number] annotators.
